      "t": "I DO not know whether you have heard of the late events which have happened to the poor unfortunate Rousseau, who has become extremely extravagant, and who is an object of the greatest compassion. It is about three weeks since he set off, without giving the least nature of it, from M. Davenport’s house, carrying with him only his gouvernante (female attendant), and leaving behind the greatest part of his effects, and about thirty guineas in money. There was also found on his table a letter full of reproaches against his host, whom he accused of having been an accomplice in a plot of mine to ruin and dishonour him. He took the road to London; and M. Davenport begged of me to search for him, and to learn how his baggage and money should be sent to him. Nothing was heard of him for a fortnight; when, at length, the Chancellor received from him a most extravagant letter, dated from Spalding in Lincolnshire. He tells this magistrate that he is on his way to Dover with the design of quitting the kingdom (observe that Spalding is quite out of the road); but that he dare not proceed one step farther, nor even go out of doors, from the fear of his enemies. He then conjures the Chancellor to send him a guide authorized to conduct him, and he requests this as the last act of hospitality of this nation toward him. A few days after, I learned from M. Davenport, that he had received a fresh letter from Rousseau dated also from Spalding, in which he testified the most lively repentance. He speaks of his sad and unfortunate situation. . . . At the end of a few hours General Conway received a letter from him, dated at Dover, about two hundred miles distant from Spalding. . . . Nothing can be more obscure than this letter. He supposes that he is a state prisoner in the hands of General Conway, and that in consequence of my suggestions. . . . He represents the danger which he runs of being assassinated. . . ."
